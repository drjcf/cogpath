# Retrobulbar Hematoma After Blepharoplasty: Teaching Summary

(Composite, de-identified teaching material for lab testing. Not a source of truth; verify against current references before clinical use.)

## Why it matters
Retrobulbar (orbital) hematoma is the most feared complication of blepharoplasty because it can progress to permanent visual loss within hours. Reported incidence is low: on the order of 1 in 2,000 cases for retrobulbar hemorrhage and roughly 1 in 10,000–20,000 for permanent blindness: but every surgeon who performs eyelid surgery must recognize and treat it without delay. Most cases declare themselves within the first 24 hours, and a large share within the first few hours after surgery.

## Mechanism
Bleeding posterior to the orbital septum raises pressure inside the closed bony orbit. Because the orbit cannot expand, rising intraorbital pressure compresses the central retinal artery and the vasa nervorum of the optic nerve and impairs venous outflow. Retinal and optic nerve ischemia become irreversible after approximately 90–120 minutes, which defines the window for intervention. Manipulation or excision of orbital fat, especially in the lower lid, is the usual source: a retracted vessel in the fat pad bleeds into the deep orbit after the lid is closed.

## Risk factors
- Hypertension, particularly uncontrolled perioperative blood pressure
- Anticoagulant and antiplatelet drugs, including aspirin, NSAIDs, fish oil, vitamin E, ginkgo, and garlic supplements
- Postoperative vomiting, coughing, straining, or bending: any Valsalva-type pressure spike
- Fat excision (transconjunctival or transcutaneous), reoperation, and prolonged or traumatic dissection
- Bleeding diatheses and liver disease

## Recognition
The classic presentation is sudden, severe, deep orbital pain out of proportion to the usual postoperative discomfort, accompanied by a tense, proptotic globe. Additional findings include rapidly expanding periorbital ecchymosis and swelling, decreased visual acuity or complete loss of light perception, a relative afferent pupillary defect, restricted extraocular movements (ophthalmoplegia), chemosis, and markedly elevated intraocular pressure (often above 40 mmHg). Superficial eyelid hematoma, by contrast, produces a soft, compressible swelling without proptosis, with preserved vision and normal pupil responses, and does not threaten sight.

Vision should be assessed at every postoperative check in the recovery area, and patients must be instructed to call immediately for severe pain, bulging, or any change in vision.

## Management
Treatment is a surgical emergency and should not wait for imaging or for the ophthalmologist to arrive.
1. Open the wound at the bedside: remove sutures and evacuate accessible clot to decompress the orbit.
2. If pressure signs persist, perform lateral canthotomy with inferior cantholysis to release the globe from the lateral orbital rim. Superior cantholysis can be added if decompression remains inadequate.
3. Adjuncts to lower intraocular pressure while definitive decompression proceeds: elevate the head of bed, apply cold compresses, give intravenous mannitol and acetazolamide, and consider high-dose intravenous corticosteroids.
4. Obtain urgent ophthalmology consultation and consider formal orbital exploration and hemostasis in the operating room for ongoing bleeding.
5. Imaging (CT orbit) is reserved for situations in which the diagnosis is uncertain and vision is intact; it must never delay decompression when vision is threatened.

## Prevention
Stop anticoagulants, antiplatelets, and relevant supplements for an appropriate interval before surgery when it is safe to do so; control blood pressure before, during, and after the procedure; use meticulous hemostasis with bipolar cautery during fat excision; avoid excessive traction on fat pads; treat nausea aggressively; and provide written instructions describing warning symptoms and how to reach the surgeon at any hour.
